# The Ten Rules: Expanded Notes

This file provides longer explanations and key passages for each of the ten rules. Read it when you need to recover the *reason* a rule exists, not just the rule. Quotations are from William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*, 25th anniversary edition.

## 1. Simplicity

> "The secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Every word that serves no function, every long word that could be a short word, every adverb that carries the same meaning that's already in the verb, every passive construction that leaves the reader unsure of who is doing what — these are the thousand and one adulterants that weaken the strength of a sentence."

The principle: an attention-stressed reader will quit at the first sentence that makes them work harder than necessary. Carelessness of any kind — clutter, hidden actors, switched tenses, broken logic — gives the reader an exit. Your job is to remove every exit.

Two practical implications:
1. **Brevity is not minimalism.** The goal is not the shortest sentence; it's the sentence with no waste. A long, hard-working sentence is fine.
2. **Cut to the verb.** When in doubt, locate the main verb and ask: does this sentence move because of the verb, or in spite of it? If the verb is "to be" or "to have," see if a real verb is hiding nearby.

## 2. Clutter

> "Clutter is the disease of American writing. We are a society strangling in unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills and meaningless jargon."

Clutter has four common shapes:
- **Inflation**: long words and phrases used where short ones would do. ("Experiencing precipitation" for "raining.")
- **Euphemism**: language used to hide a meaning rather than convey one. ("Volume reduction unit" for "dump.")
- **Padding**: prepositions, qualifiers, and clusters that add no information. ("Head up," "a bit," "It is interesting to note.")
- **Jargon**: in-group language used to sound expert. ("Leverage," "paradigm," "ecosystem.")

Zinsser's diagnostic technique, "bracketing," is the one habit worth internalizing. As you read your draft, put mental brackets around any word or phrase that isn't doing useful work. Then read the sentence without it. If it still says what you meant, delete.

The full clutter hit list, with replacements, is in [clutter.md](clutter.md).

## 3. Clear Thinking → Clear Writing

> "Clear thinking becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other. It's impossible for a muddy thinker to write good English."

The implication is uncomfortable: if you can't write the sentence cleanly, you don't yet know what you mean. Editing isn't decorative; it's a thinking tool. Watch for sentences that hide imprecision under abstraction or under multiple clauses. Those sentences mark the spots where the thought is still unfinished.

Two questions to ask sentence by sentence:
1. *What am I trying to say?*
2. *Have I said it? Is it clear to someone seeing the subject for the first time?*

## 4. Use "I." Sound Like a Human.

> "Writing is an intimate transaction between two people, conducted on paper, and it will go well to the extent that it retains its humanity. Therefore I urge people to write in the first person."

Many contexts forbid first person (news, formal reports, dissertations). Zinsser concedes this. His harder claim: even where "I" is forbidden, write as if "I" is present. The columnist James Reston never used "I" yet his personality saturated his columns. The trick is to keep the human voice — opinion, specific detail, unmistakable cadence — even when the pronoun is unavailable.

The opposite failure mode is the institutional voice: "Initiatives have been undertaken," "Priorities have been identified," "It is hoped that…" These sentences are produced by no one, addressed to no one, and remembered by no one. When you catch yourself writing them, ask: *who is speaking, and to whom?*

## 5. Style Is the Person. Don't Reach for It.

> "Style is organic to the person doing the writing, as much a part of him as his hair, or, if he is bald, his lack of it. Trying to add style is like adding a toupee."

The most common failure of new writers is reaching for "style" — gaudy similes, fancy adjectives, archaic vocabulary, ostentatious sentence rhythms. The reader sees the reach and trusts the writer less. Tom Wolfe writes Tom Wolfe sentences because he is Tom Wolfe; you are not Tom Wolfe.

The practical rule: build sturdy sentences first. Voice is what remains when you've cut everything that isn't yours.

## 6. Verbs

> "Use active verbs unless there is no comfortable way to get around using a passive verb. The difference between an active-verb style and a passive-verb style — in clarity and vigor — is the difference between life and death for a writer."

Active verbs name the actor. Passive verbs hide them. Compare:
- "The decision was made to discontinue the program." (Who decided?)
- "The board cut the program." (Six words. Specific.)

The same logic applies one level down: concrete nouns beat abstract ones. "Three customers complained" is more honest, more specific, and more memorable than "stakeholder feedback was negative."

Two related rules:
- **Distrust adverbs.** "Smile happily" is one word too long. The verb already contains the smile's affect.
- **Distrust most adjectives.** Adjectives that state the obvious ("tall skyscraper") or duplicate the noun's meaning ("personal opinion") are clutter. Adjectives that genuinely modify ("a *small* skyscraper") earn their keep.

## 7. Unity

> "Unity is the anchor of good writing. So, first, get your unities straight."

Before drafting, decide:
- **Pronoun**: first, second, or third person? One choice. Hold it.
- **Tense**: past, present, or hypothetical? Switching tenses without intent is a tell.
- **Mood**: casual, formal, ironic, reverent, plainspoken? Pick one frame.
- **Attitude toward the material**: are you a participant, a witness, a judge, an enthusiast? Don't change roles mid-piece.

Zinsser's example — a travel writer who lurches from "Ann and I had always wanted to visit Hong Kong" into "Hong Kong affords many fascinating experiences to the curious sightseer" — is the canonical failure. The writer hasn't decided who is speaking. The reader notices.

## 8. Think Small

> "Nobody can write a book or an article 'about' something. Tolstoy couldn't write a book about war and peace, or Melville a book about whaling. They made certain reductive decisions about time and place and about individual characters in that time and place — one man pursuing one whale."

Every piece of nonfiction should leave the reader with one provocative thought they didn't have before. Not two. Not five. One.

Practically: before drafting, write the one sentence you want the reader to walk away with. Tape it to the top of the page. If a paragraph doesn't earn its keep relative to that sentence, cut it.

## 9. The Lead and the Ending

> "The most important sentence in any article is the first one. If it doesn't induce the reader to proceed to the second sentence, your article is dead."

A working lead does at least one of these:
- Drops the reader inside a specific moment.
- Offers a fact strange enough to demand explanation.
- Names a person and shows them doing something.
- Promises something only this piece can deliver.

What a lead should not do:
- Set the scene with weather, the season, or generic context.
- Announce its own subject ("In this piece I'd like to discuss…").
- Open with the broadest possible generalization ("Throughout human history…").

On endings:
> "The perfect ending should take your readers slightly by surprise and yet seem exactly right. They didn't expect the article to end so soon, or so abruptly, or to say what it said. But they know it when they see it."

A good ending often turns. It ends on an image, a quotation, a small reversal, a return to the lead. It does *not* fractally summarize what you just said. If your last paragraph starts with "In conclusion" or "To sum up" or "All in all," delete it.

## 10. Rewriting

> "Rewriting is the essence of writing well: it's where the game is won or lost."

The first draft is raw material. Treat it as such. Zinsser's manuscript pages — reproduced in the book — show pages that have been retyped four or five times, then revised again. The point: the prose you admire is the result of cuts you don't see.

The minimum rewriting pass:
1. Read each paragraph aloud.
2. Cut every word that isn't doing new work.
3. Look at paragraph 1. Could the piece start at paragraph 2 or 3? Usually yes.
4. Look at the last paragraph. Could the piece end one sentence earlier? Usually yes.
5. Check the verbs. Replace at least one limp verb per paragraph.

Two extensions:
- **Cut by 50%.** Zinsser claims most first drafts can be halved without losing information or voice. Try it as an exercise even when you won't keep the result — the discipline reveals which sentences you actually need.
- **Trust the material if it pulls you off course.** Sometimes the act of writing reveals you wanted to say something else. Follow the new direction, then go back and make the front match the back.

## A Note on Voice

> "Develop one voice that readers will recognize when they hear it on the page."

Zinsser wrote a book on baseball and a book on jazz and refused to use "sports English" for one and "jazz English" for the other. His commodity, as he said, was him. The same applies to writing on behalf of a user: maintain one voice across topics. Don't shape-shift. The user's voice is not "casual for emails, formal for memos" — it's a single voice operating across registers.

## A Note on Enjoyment

> "If you would like to write better than everybody else, you have to want to write better than everybody else. You must take an obsessive pride in the smallest details of your craft."

This is the closing argument of the book and the foundation of everything else. The rules are simple. The discipline is to apply them every time, even on routine prose. Especially on routine prose — that's where the slop builds.
