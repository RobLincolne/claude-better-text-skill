# The Ten Rules: Reasoning

Longer notes on why each rule exists. Read this when a rule needs a judgment call rather than a lookup. Source: William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*, 25th anniversary edition. The wording is our own.

## 1. Cut every word that does no work.

Zinsser's central claim is that good writing comes from stripping each sentence to its cleanest components. His list of adulterants: words doing no work, long words standing where short ones serve, adverbs repeating what the verb carries, passive constructions that hide who acted.

The mechanism behind the rule is reader attention. A reader working harder than the sentence requires stops reading. Clutter, hidden actors, switched tenses, and broken logic each give the reader a place to quit.

Two implications:

1. **The target is waste.** A long sentence that works throughout survives the pass. A six-word sentence carrying three dead words does not.
2. **Check the main verb first.** Where the main verb is "to be" or "to have," a real verb is usually hiding in the sentence as a noun.

## 2. Delete clutter by category.

Zinsser diagnoses clutter as the disease of American writing: unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills, and jargon that carries no meaning.

Four shapes:

- **Inflation.** Long words and phrases where short ones serve. "Experiencing precipitation" for "raining."
- **Euphemism.** Language chosen to hide a meaning. "Volume reduction unit" for "dump."
- **Padding.** Prepositions, qualifiers, and clusters that add no information. "Head up," "a bit," "It is interesting to note."
- **Jargon.** In-group language used to signal expertise. "Leverage," "paradigm," "ecosystem."

The habit worth internalizing is bracketing. As you read a draft, put mental brackets around anything not doing useful work, then read the sentence without it. Where the meaning holds, delete.

## 3. Rewrite the sentence you cannot write cleanly.

Zinsser ties the two together: clear writing follows from clear thinking, and neither survives without the other.

The working consequence is a diagnostic. A sentence that will not come out clean marks a thought that is not finished, so editing the words will not fix it. Abstraction and stacked clauses are where this hides. Those sentences mark the spots to think through again.

Two questions, sentence by sentence:

1. What am I trying to say?
2. Have I said it, and would a reader new to the subject understand it?

## 4. Name the speaker.

Zinsser describes writing as a transaction between two people conducted on paper, which succeeds to the degree it stays human. That is his argument for the first person.

Many contexts forbid "I": news, formal reports, dissertations. Zinsser concedes the constraint and makes a harder claim, that the writer should stay present anyway. James Reston never used "I" and his personality still ran through every column. The markers that survive the ban are stated opinion, specific detail, and a recognizable cadence.

The failure mode is the institutional voice. "Initiatives have been undertaken." "Priorities have been identified." "It is hoped that…" No actor appears in any of them, so the reader learns nothing about who did what. When one of these turns up in a draft, ask who is speaking and to whom.

## 5. Do not decorate.

Zinsser argues that style is organic to the writer, and that reaching for it deliberately reads like a toupee.

New writers reach hardest: gaudy similes, fancy adjectives, archaic vocabulary, ostentatious rhythms. The reader sees the reach and trusts the writer less. Tom Wolfe writes Tom Wolfe sentences because he is Tom Wolfe.

The practical rule is order of operations. Build sturdy sentences first. What remains after the cuts is the voice.

## 6. Put the work in the verbs.

Zinsser's rule: use active verbs unless there is no comfortable way around a passive one. He rates the gap between an active-verb style and a passive-verb style as the largest single difference in clarity and vigor available to a writer.

Active verbs name the actor and passive verbs hide them.

- "The decision was made to discontinue the program." Who decided?
- "The board cut the program." Six words, one actor, one action.

The same logic runs one level down into nouns. "Three customers complained" is more specific and more memorable than "stakeholder feedback was negative."

Two related rules:

- **Adverbs.** "Smile happily" is one word too long. The verb already carries the affect.
- **Adjectives.** Cut the ones that state the obvious ("tall skyscraper") or duplicate the noun ("personal opinion"). Keep the ones that change the meaning ("a *small* skyscraper").

## 7. Hold one pronoun, tense, and register.

Zinsser calls unity the anchor of good writing and says to settle the unities before starting.

Four decisions, made before drafting:

- **Pronoun.** First, second, or third person.
- **Tense.** Past, present, or hypothetical. An unintended switch is a tell.
- **Mood.** Casual, formal, ironic, plainspoken.
- **Stance toward the material.** Participant, witness, judge, or enthusiast.

Zinsser's example is a travel writer moving from "Ann and I had always wanted to visit Hong Kong" to "Hong Kong affords many fascinating experiences to the curious sightseer." The writer has not decided who is speaking, and the reader hears both.

## 8. Make one point.

Nobody writes a book or an article *about* a subject, in Zinsser's account. Tolstoy did not write about war and peace. Each writer makes reductive choices: a time, a place, particular people inside it.

A piece of nonfiction should leave the reader with one thought they did not have before.

The practical step is to write that sentence down before drafting and keep it in view. Any paragraph that does not earn its place against that sentence goes.

## 9. Open with the news. Stop at the last fact.

For Zinsser the first sentence is the most important one in the piece, because a reader who does not reach the second sentence never reaches any of them.

A working lead does at least one of these:

- Drops the reader inside a specific moment.
- States a fact strange enough to demand explanation.
- Names a person and shows them doing something.
- Promises something only this piece delivers.

A lead should not set the scene with weather or season, announce its own subject ("In this piece I'd like to discuss…"), or open on the broadest available generalization ("Throughout human history…").

On endings, Zinsser wants one that arrives sooner than the reader expects and still lands as right. A good ending often turns: an image, a quotation, a small reversal, a return to the lead. It does not summarize the piece. Where the last paragraph opens with "In conclusion," "To sum up," or "All in all," delete the paragraph.

## 10. Revise before delivering.

Zinsser puts rewriting at the centre of the craft. His manuscript pages, reproduced in the book, show drafts retyped four or five times and then revised again.

The minimum pass:

1. Read each paragraph aloud.
2. Cut every word not doing new work.
3. Check paragraph 1. Could the piece start at paragraph 2 or 3? Usually yes.
4. Check the last paragraph. Could the piece end one sentence earlier? Usually yes.
5. Replace at least one limp verb per paragraph.

Two extensions:

- **Cut by 50% as an exercise.** Zinsser claims most first drafts survive it. Run the cut even where you will not keep the result, because it shows which sentences the piece actually needs.
- **Follow the material if it moves.** Writing sometimes reveals a different point than the one planned. Take the new direction, then rewrite the front to match the back.

## Note on Voice

Zinsser wrote a book on baseball and a book on jazz and refused to use "sports English" for one and "jazz English" for the other. His commodity, in his words, was him.

The same holds when writing on behalf of a user. Hold one voice across topics and let it operate across registers, so the formal memo and the casual email still read as the same person.

## Note on Routine Prose

The rules are easy to state and easy to skip on low-stakes work. Routine prose is where the slop accumulates, so run the pass on the short status email as well as the long essay.
